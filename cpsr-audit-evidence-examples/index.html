CPSR audit evidence examples

CPSR audit evidence examples are the documents, records, and working papers that show a supplier has assessed compliance with UK defence procurement requirements. They demonstrate how a company checks risk, ownership, controls, and commercial readiness before bids or contract award. Strong evidence is current, traceable, and tied to a specific policy, process, or transaction.

When a reviewer asks for evidence, they are usually looking for proof that a control exists and is actually used, not just a written statement that it should exist. The most useful examples are those that show the full path from policy to action: who approved something, what was checked, when it was checked, and what happened next.

  • Policies and procedures that define the control
  • Completed records that show the control was performed
  • Approvals, sign-offs, or exception notes that explain decisions
  • Registers, logs, and reconciliations that support consistency
  • Training, org charts, and role descriptions that show accountability

Good evidence is usually specific enough that an independent reviewer can follow the trail without having to guess. A statement such as “we review contracts regularly” is weak on its own; a dated contract review, with comments, actions, and sign-off, is much stronger.

CPSR audit evidence examples for contract file control

Contract file evidence shows that each contract is created, stored, and maintained in a controlled way. Typical examples include the signed contract, amendments, purchase orders, correspondence on scope changes, and an indexed file showing version history. These records help prove the contract terms can be reconstructed accurately.

For contract file control, reviewers often want to see that the file is complete and organized. That can include the final signed agreement, all executed modifications, key emails confirming scope or pricing changes, and an index showing the document set is current.

A strong file also shows traceability. If a change was made to delivery dates, pricing, or technical scope, the evidence should include the reason for the change, who approved it, and the resulting revision to the contract or order record.

If a contract is managed across multiple systems, the evidence should explain where the authoritative record sits. For example, a contract register can point to the master file, while supporting emails or minutes show the commercial approval trail.

CPSR audit evidence examples for pricing and cost buildup

Pricing evidence shows how a quoted or proposed price was built, reviewed, and approved. Useful examples include cost worksheets, supplier quotes, labor rate calculations, overhead or burden schedules, and management approval of the final price. The best evidence links the final price to its source data.

Pricing evidence is often judged on whether it can be traced back to reliable inputs. A reviewer may expect to see labor categories, estimated hours, material assumptions, subcontractor quotes, and any escalation or adjustment logic used in the proposal.

If the business uses standard rates, the evidence should show how those rates were established and when they were last updated. If the estimate uses assumptions, those assumptions should be written down clearly so the reviewer can see what was included and what was excluded.

Where a price approval process exists, keep the signed approval or system workflow record. That helps show the price was reviewed by someone with authority, rather than being entered without oversight.

CPSR audit evidence examples for purchasing and subcontract oversight

Purchasing and subcontract evidence shows that suppliers are selected, evaluated, and managed using consistent controls. Examples include approved vendor lists, quotation comparisons, purchase requisitions, subcontract agreements, and performance or delivery reviews. These records show how the company supports cost, quality, and compliance decisions.

In purchasing, the reviewer may look for evidence that competition was sought where appropriate or that the sole-source decision was justified. Quotation tabs, buyer notes, and approval memos can be useful if they show why one supplier was selected over another.

For subcontract work, the evidence should show the scope, price, delivery terms, and any flowdown requirements. If the subcontract carries special obligations, such as quality or reporting terms, those terms should appear in the agreement or in a linked compliance checklist.

Supplier monitoring evidence is also important. Delivery performance reports, quality issue logs, and corrective action records can show that suppliers are not only selected properly but also managed after award.

CPSR audit evidence examples for timekeeping and labor charging

Timekeeping evidence shows that labor is recorded accurately and charged to the correct contract or indirect account. Typical examples include signed timecards, electronic time entries, labor distribution reports, and correction logs. The evidence should show timely entry, review, and approval.

Labor charging controls are often tested because errors here can affect many contracts at once. Strong evidence usually includes the employee’s time record, the approving manager’s review, and the payroll or job-cost distribution report that reflects the entered time.

If corrections are made, the audit trail matters. A late change should show the original entry, the reason for the adjustment, who made the correction, and who approved it if approval is required by policy.

Training records can support this area as well. If employees are trained on timekeeping rules, a dated attendance log or system completion record helps show that the policy is not just written down but communicated to staff.

CPSR audit evidence examples for purchasing system compliance and approvals

Approval evidence shows that transactions move through the right authorization path. Examples include delegation-of-authority matrices, workflow approvals, signature logs, and exception approvals for unusual transactions. These records prove that people acted within their assigned authority.

A good approval trail should identify the approver, the date, the transaction value, and the type of action approved. If thresholds change by dollar amount or contract type, the matrix should be current and easy to match to the transaction being tested.

Exception handling is especially important. If a buyer bypassed a normal step because of urgency, the file should explain why, who allowed the exception, and how the issue was resolved later. That kind of record shows control discipline rather than hidden noncompliance.

System screenshots can help, but they should not be the only evidence if a transaction exists in a live record. A workflow log, approval email, or audit trail export is usually easier to rely on because it is time-stamped and harder to misread.

CPSR audit evidence examples for what makes documentation persuasive

Persuasive evidence is complete, dated, and linked to the specific control being tested. It should show who did what, when they did it, what source data they used, and what decision was made. Evidence is strongest when the reviewer can follow the trail from policy to record to outcome.

Documentation becomes more persuasive when it is consistent across samples. If one contract file has a complete approval trail and another is missing key steps, the inconsistency can raise questions even if the missing step was performed verbally.

One practical approach is to keep evidence in layers: policy, procedure, record, and summary. The policy defines the rule, the procedure explains how it works, the record proves it happened, and the summary helps the reviewer understand the logic quickly.

If a document has a known weakness, such as a missing signature or a late entry, do not hide it. A short explanation showing the issue, the corrective action, and the prevention step is usually better than a file that appears complete but cannot be reconciled with other records.

For anyone preparing for review, the goal is not to produce the largest stack of papers. The goal is to provide evidence that is relevant, current, and easy to trace back to the underlying control. That is what makes audit support credible and efficient.
