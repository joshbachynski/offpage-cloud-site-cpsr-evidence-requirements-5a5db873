CPSR evidence checklist for a complete and review-ready submission

CPSR evidence checklist is a structured list of documents, records, and product details used to show that a cosmetic product is suitable for assessment under the Cosmetic Product Safety Report process. It helps a safety assessor verify composition, manufacturing, packaging, labeling, stability, microbiology, and exposure information. A complete checklist reduces gaps that can delay the report or trigger follow-up questions.

Use this page as a practical guide when preparing evidence for a cosmetic product safety review. The goal is not just to collect paperwork, but to assemble information that supports a clear safety rationale. Every item should help the assessor understand what the product is, how it is made, how it is used, and why it is expected to be safe under normal or reasonably foreseeable use.

  • Product identity and variant details
  • Full qualitative and quantitative formula
  • Raw material specifications and safety data
  • Packaging and contact material details
  • Microbiological, stability, and compatibility evidence
  • Label, claims, and intended use information
  • Manufacturing and batch traceability records

CPSR evidence checklist for product identity and formula details

Product identity evidence tells the assessor exactly what is being reviewed. The formula evidence must show every ingredient, concentration, and function so the safety assessment can be tied to a specific finished product. If the product has several variants, each variant needs clear identification.

Start with the basics: product name, brand name, product type, shade or fragrance variant, and the exact version being assessed. If the product has multiple pack sizes or regional versions, note whether the formula is identical or whether any ingredients differ. A small difference in fragrance load, colorant level, or preservative system can change the safety profile.

The formula should be presented in a clear quantitative format, ideally with percentages that add to 100% for the finished product. Each ingredient should be named consistently, with the trade name and the relevant technical name where available. The assessor needs enough detail to identify each substance and understand the function it plays in the product.

Include the intended cosmetic use and the application site. A leave-on face cream, rinse-off shampoo, lip product, and eye-area product all create different exposure considerations. The same ingredient may be acceptable at one level in one use scenario and require more scrutiny in another.

CPSR evidence checklist for ingredient safety and supplier documentation

Ingredient safety evidence shows that each material has a known composition and an acceptable quality profile. Supplier documents should support identity, purity, and any relevant restrictions or impurities. Safety assessors use these records to judge whether the finished product formula is well controlled.

Collect technical data sheets, specifications, certificates of analysis, and safety data sheets where relevant. These documents help confirm the composition and quality of raw materials. They are especially important for botanical extracts, surfactants, fragrance materials, colorants, and multifunctional ingredients that may vary by supplier or batch.

If an ingredient has known impurities, allergens, residual solvents, heavy metals, or microbiological concerns, the evidence should address them directly. For example, an extract may need information on solvent residues, or a fragrance component may need allergen declarations. The assessor is looking for evidence that the supplier has controlled foreseeable hazards, not just a product name on an invoice.

When possible, keep supplier documentation aligned to the exact raw material used in the finished product. Substituting one grade for another without updating the evidence can create gaps in the report. If a supplier changes manufacturing site or specification, the supporting evidence should be refreshed before the CPSR is finalized.

CPSR evidence checklist for packaging, stability, and microbiology

Packaging, stability, and microbiology evidence shows that the product remains safe and fit for use over its shelf life. The assessor uses this information to judge whether the formula stays within acceptable limits and whether the pack protects the product from contamination or degradation. These records are essential for water-containing or preservative-dependent products.

Packaging evidence should identify the primary container, dispensing system, closure, and any inner components that contact the product. Materials such as plastic, glass, coated metal, or pump components can affect compatibility. The assessor may need to know whether the pack could react with oils, solvents, acids, or fragrance-rich formulas.

Stability information should show that the product maintains appearance, odor, viscosity, and functional performance over the intended shelf life. Even a simple evidence package should explain the conditions tested, the duration, and any observed changes. If a product is sensitive to heat, light, or freeze-thaw cycling, that should be documented so the report can reflect realistic storage conditions.

Microbiological evidence is particularly important for aqueous products, products used near the eyes, and products with a higher contamination risk during consumer use. A product that contains water and supports microbial growth generally needs stronger preservation evidence than an anhydrous balm or powder. Preserve challenge testing, microbial limits testing, and batch hygiene controls all help show that the product can remain safe throughout its life.

CPSR evidence checklist for manufacturing controls and batch traceability

Manufacturing evidence shows how the finished product is produced, controlled, and traced. The assessor needs confidence that the formula on paper matches the product made in practice. Batch traceability helps connect every finished unit back to a controlled production record.

Provide a manufacturing flow or batch record summary that explains how the product is weighed, mixed, filled, and packaged. The record should show where quality checks happen and how nonconforming material is handled. If the process includes heating, cooling, filtration, or de-aeration, that information can matter to product consistency and safety.

Keep records for batch numbers, production dates, and release decisions. These details do not just support compliance; they also make it easier to investigate complaints or recalls if a problem ever arises. Clear traceability is one of the strongest indicators that the final product is being managed responsibly.

If manufacturing is outsourced, make sure the contract manufacturer’s records are available and consistent with the finished formula. The safety assessor may need evidence that the site follows appropriate quality controls and that the product is produced under a repeatable process. Incomplete manufacturing documentation is one of the most common reasons that a safety review stalls.

CPSR evidence checklist for labeling, claims, and use scenario support

Label and claim evidence helps confirm how the product is presented to consumers and how it is intended to be used. Claims can affect the safety assessment if they imply special use, stronger exposure, or an unintended product category. The label should match the formula, function, and warning statements.

Include draft artwork or a final label copy showing ingredients, nominal content, warnings, batch identification, durability information, and any mandatory statements. If the product is for external use only, keep that wording consistent across documents. If the pack includes directions such as “avoid the eye area” or “patch test before use,” those instructions should be supported by the product’s risk profile.

Claims should be reviewed carefully because they can change the way a product is interpreted. A moisturizer is not assessed the same way as a product that claims to be intense, long-lasting, ultra-strong, or designed for very sensitive skin. Claims should be realistic, consistent with the formula, and not create an impression of medical benefit unless that is clearly outside the cosmetic scope.

Also include the expected user group and frequency of use. A product intended for children, frequent reapplication, or use on damaged skin may require closer scrutiny than a standard adult consumer product. The more clearly the evidence describes normal use, the easier it is for the assessor to match the safety reasoning to the real-world product.

CPSR evidence checklist for organizing a submission that moves smoothly

A well-organized evidence pack lets the assessor verify facts quickly and spot missing items early. The most useful submission is complete, version-controlled, and easy to cross-reference. Good organization saves time and lowers the risk of repeated clarification requests.

Create a document index that lists every file, its version, its date, and what it supports in the assessment. Put the formula at the center of the pack and make sure supplier documents, packaging details, stability data, and labels all point back to the same product version. If one document refers to an older formula, flag it before submission.

Review the evidence for consistency in names, units, and dates. A mismatch between the formula sheet, label artwork, and supplier specification can raise avoidable questions. If information is missing, note it openly and explain whether it will be provided later, replaced, or confirmed by the manufacturer.

Above all, treat the checklist as a quality-control tool rather than a paperwork exercise. Strong evidence makes the assessor’s job easier and gives the finished product a better foundation for a confident safety conclusion. When the records are complete and aligned, the final report can focus on real risk evaluation instead of correcting basic omissions.

Useful evidence is specific, current, and consistent across every document in the submission. If one detail changes, update all related records before sending the pack for review.
